# The model

This page gives the full mathematical description of the finite-basis Wishart
process implemented by `wishart-process-em`. It follows the model of
[Nejatbakhsh, Garon & Williams (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11824)
("Estimating Noise Correlations Across Continuous Conditions With Wishart
Processes", *NeurIPS 2023*), with a finite Fourier-feature basis and an
EM-style, marginal-likelihood inference in place of variational inference.

## Problem setup

We record the activity of $N$ neurons across $C$ experimental conditions
indexed by a continuous parameter $x \in [0,1]^D$ (e.g. grating orientation,
reach angle, tone frequency). At each condition we observe a small number $K$
of trials. Writing $y_{ck} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ for trial $k$ at condition $c$, the
object of interest is the **noise covariance**

$$
\Sigma(x) \;=\; \operatorname{Cov}\!\big[\, y \mid x \,\big],
$$

i.e. the trial-to-trial (co)variability of the population at each condition.

The statistical difficulty is the **$K < N$ regime**: with fewer trials per
condition than neurons, the per-condition sample covariance is rank-deficient
and badly conditioned, so it cannot be estimated condition-by-condition. The
Wishart process resolves this by assuming $\Sigma(x)$ varies *smoothly* in $x$,
so trials at neighbouring conditions inform each other and statistical power is
pooled across conditions.

## Generative model

Each trial is generated by first drawing a low-dimensional standard-normal
latent, mapping it through a condition-dependent mean and covariance square
root, and finally passing the result through an observation model:

$$
v \sim \mathcal N(0, I_Q), \qquad
\eta \;=\; \mu(x) + G(x)\, v, \qquad
y \sim p(\,\cdot \mid \eta),
$$

where $\eta \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is a **latent linear predictor**, $\mu(x)$ is a
smooth mean function, and $G(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times Q}$ is a covariance
square root satisfying

$$
G(x)\, G(x)^\top \;=\; \Sigma(x).
$$

For the Gaussian observation model the latent $v$ integrates out analytically
and $y \sim \mathcal N(\mu(x), \Sigma(x))$ exactly; for count models the latent
is integrated numerically (see [below](#marginal-likelihood-estimators)).

## Weight-space Gaussian-process basis

Both the mean function $\mu(x)$ and the covariance factors are built from scalar
Gaussian processes. Rather than working with a full kernel matrix, each scalar
GP is approximated in *weight space* by a **truncated Fourier expansion**:

$$
f(x) \;=\; \sum_{k} \tau_k \big( w^{\sin}_k \sin(2\pi\, k \cdot x)
                               + w^{\cos}_k \cos(2\pi\, k \cdot x) \big),
\qquad w \sim \mathcal N(0, 1),
$$

where the integer frequency vectors $k$ range over a grid (excluding the
constant mode, which is carried by an explicit bias term) and the feature
amplitudes are

$$
\tau_k \;=\; \sqrt{S(2\pi k)},
$$

with $S(\omega)$ the **spectral density** of the desired kernel. By Bochner's
theorem, a stationary kernel $k(x,x')$ has a spectral density given by the
Fourier transform of $k$; weighting each Fourier feature by $\sqrt{S(\omega)}$
and placing a standard-normal prior on the weights therefore reproduces the
corresponding stationary GP prior on $f$. Two spectral densities are provided:

- **Squared-exponential (RBF):** $S(\omega) \propto \exp(-\tfrac12 \ell^2
  |\omega|^2)$. The length scale $\ell$ is the smoothness / bandwidth
  hyperparameter (the analogue of $\lambda$ in the paper).
- **Matérn** with smoothness $\nu$: $S(\omega) \propto (2\nu/\ell^2 +
  |\omega|^2)^{-(\nu + 1/2)}$; $\nu \to \infty$ recovers the squared
  exponential.

Working in weight space turns the otherwise nonparametric GP into a finite,
differentiable parametric model whose free parameters are just the weights $w$,
on which we place a standard-normal prior. This is exactly what makes the
marginal-likelihood / EM inference tractable.

!!! note "Periodicity"
    Inputs $x$ are assumed scaled to the unit box $[0,1]^D$. Because the
    features are Fourier modes on the torus, **periodic** conditions (grating
    orientation, reach angle) wrap around naturally. Non-periodic conditions
    should be mapped into $[0,1]$ with a little headroom at the edges.

## The covariance family

The covariance follows the full family of the paper (sec. 2.3):

$$
\Sigma(x) \;=\; L\,\big( U(x)\, U(x)^\top + \Lambda(x) \big)\, L^\top,
$$

with three ingredients, each optional beyond the low-rank core:

- **Low-rank factor** $U(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times P}$. Its columns are
  weight-space GPs; column $p$ is scaled by a learnable per-latent (ARD) scale
  $s^Z_p = \operatorname{softplus}(\cdot) > 0$, so $U(x) = A(x)\,
  \operatorname{diag}(s^Z)$ with $A(x)$ the raw GP factor. The rank $P$ controls
  how many shared covariance modes are modelled.
- **Diagonal term** $\Lambda(x) = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda(x))$, optional
  (the *low-rank plus diagonal* / `WPlrd` extension). Each entry
  $\lambda_n(x) = \operatorname{softplus}(g_n(x)) \ge 0$ is a
  softplus-transformed GP, adding per-neuron heteroscedastic variance.
- **Scale matrix** $L$, an optional learnable lower-triangular matrix capturing
  condition-independent covariance. It is typically initialised from the
  Cholesky factor of the *grand empirical covariance* (pooled, within-condition
  centred). Its diagonal is passed through softplus to guarantee a valid
  Cholesky factor.

For sampling and for the non-Gaussian marginal likelihood we use the
square-root factor $G(x)$ directly:

$$
G(x) \;=\; L \begin{bmatrix} U(x) & \operatorname{diag}\!\big(\sqrt{\lambda(x)}\big) \end{bmatrix},
\qquad \Sigma(x) = G(x) G(x)^\top + \epsilon I,
$$

with a small jitter $\epsilon$ for numerical stability. The **latent dimension**
is

$$
Q \;=\; P \;+\; \begin{cases} N & \text{if the diagonal term is used,} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

When the diagonal is disabled, $Q = P$ is small, which is what keeps the QMC
latent integral efficient for count models.

## Observation models

Given the latent predictor $\eta = \mu(x) + G(x)\,v$, a likelihood maps $\eta$
to observations $y$. Three models are provided.

### Gaussian (conjugate)

$$
y \sim \mathcal N\big(\mu(x),\; \Sigma(x)\big).
$$

This is *conjugate*: the latent $v$ integrates out in closed form, so the
marginal likelihood is exactly a multivariate normal and **no Monte-Carlo is
required**. An optional homoscedastic observation-noise variance
$\sigma^2_{\text{obs}}$ can be added on the diagonal of $\Sigma(x)$. This is the
model used throughout the original paper on (pre-processed) neural data.

### Poisson

$$
y_n \sim \operatorname{Poisson}\big(\operatorname{softplus}(\eta_n)\big).
$$

The softplus inverse-link keeps rates positive while behaving linearly for large
$\eta$ (unlike $\exp$, which can overflow). Suited to raw spike counts.
Non-conjugate: the latent $v$ must be integrated numerically.

### Negative binomial

$$
y_n \sim \operatorname{NB}\big(\text{mean} = \operatorname{softplus}(\eta_n),\; r_n\big),
$$

with a per-neuron dispersion $r_n > 0$ (a weak standard-normal prior is placed
on $\log r_n$). The mean/dispersion parameterisation gives variance $\mu +
\mu^2/r$; as $r \to \infty$ the model approaches the Poisson. Captures
over-dispersed counts. Also non-conjugate.

## Marginal-likelihood estimators

Fitting maximises the **marginal** likelihood, in which the per-trial latent $v$
is integrated out:

$$
p(y \mid x, \theta) \;=\; \int p\big(y \mid \mu(x) + G(x)\,v\big)\,
                               \mathcal N(v; 0, I_Q)\, \mathrm d v .
$$

The full objective is the penalised marginal log-likelihood over the dataset,

$$
\mathcal L(\theta) \;=\; \sum_{c,k} \log p(y_{ck} \mid x_c, \theta)
                         \;+\; \log p(\theta),
$$

where $\log p(\theta)$ is the standard-normal prior on the GP weights (plus any
likelihood-parameter prior). It is maximised with Adam via `optax`. Three
per-trial estimators of $\log p(y \mid x, \theta)$ are available.

### Conjugate

For the Gaussian likelihood the integral is exact,

$$
\log p(y \mid x, \theta) \;=\; \log \mathcal N\big(y;\; \mu(x),\; \Sigma(x) + \sigma^2_{\text{obs}} I\big),
$$

so the objective is deterministic and no lattice is needed.

### QMC

For count models we draw the latent $v$ from the prior using a **randomised
rank-1 Korobov lattice**, form $\eta_s = \mu(x) + G(x)\, v_s$, and take an
importance-weighted (IWAE-style) estimate of the log marginal:

$$
\widehat{\log p}(y \mid x, \theta)
   \;=\; \operatorname*{logsumexp}_{s=1}^{S} \log p(y \mid \eta_s) \;-\; \log S .
$$

The lattice is deterministic on the unit cube but at integration time receives a
random shift (mod 1), a random rotation, and is mapped to standard normals via
the inverse Gaussian CDF; this makes the estimator **unbiased** after
randomisation while converging markedly faster than plain Monte-Carlo for smooth
integrands. A fresh randomisation each step gives a **stochastic-EM**–style
optimisation. This is the cheap, differentiable objective used during training.

### Laplace

For accurate *evaluation* (e.g. held-out log-likelihood) a lower-variance
**multiple-importance-sampling** estimate is available. Its proposal is an equal
mixture of the prior $\mathcal N(0, I)$ and a Laplace approximation $\mathcal
N(m, H^{-1})$ to the latent posterior, where the mode $m$ and Hessian $H$ come
from a per-trial damped-Newton solve; samples are combined with the balance
heuristic. This costs a Newton solve per trial, so it is intended for evaluation
rather than every gradient step. The Newton routine returns the Cholesky factor
$L$ of $H$ ($L L^\top = H$), so a Laplace sample is $m + L^{-\top}\varepsilon$
with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$.
